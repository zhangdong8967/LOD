Introduction

Fixed worksheets and about 20 replicates remain common when laboratories verify low-end analytical performance or reagent-lot changes, even if the written claims differ. CLSI EP17-A2 frames verification of detection capability, including the limit of detection (LoD), and CLSI EP26 covers acceptance of a new reagent lot.[1,2] Neither guide supplies one powered sample size for every procedure. Manufacturers often use about 60 replicates to establish LoD, whereas laboratories commonly verify with about 20 (Armbruster and Pry).[3] That manufacturer-laboratory contrast describes practice; it is not a claim-specific design rule. We limit the analysis to binary hit-rate claims. Each replicate is scored detected or not at a verification concentration. Blank-standard-deviation LoB/LoD pathways, probit or logit concentration estimation, and EP26 quantitative lot-difference tests fall outside this sizing problem.

An LoD is commonly the concentration tied to a specified detection probability, often 95%.[1,3-5] Assay performance and study operating characteristics are separate quantities. Whether a finite verification run supports or rejects the written claim depends on sample size, the acceptance rule, the nominal error rate, and the alternative detection probability. Linnet and Kondratovich treated LoD estimation when Gaussian blank assumptions fail.[4] Vaks et al. showed that pass probability for claimed-LoD verification depends on replicate number, and that failure to reject a claim does not prove the claimed LoD has been met.[5] Their setting was single-method confirmation; two-arm hit-rate comparisons between lots or reagents were not enumerated.

Protocol language that a new lot is “equivalent” to a reference lot at the claimed LoD is a comparison of detection probabilities. It is neither a single-arm 95% benchmark nor a 19-of-20 pass rule. If the true rates were 90% and 80%, exact Fisher power at 20 replicates per arm is about 0.07, and about 199 replicates per arm are needed for 80% power. A 19-of-20 positivity rule likewise scores an observed count, not confirmation of a 95% detection probability. Quantitative reagent-lot studies have already shown that fixed laboratory protocols can miss lot differences, and that EP26-type requirements vary by analyte.[6-8] We therefore enumerated exact operating characteristics for conventional n=20 on a 50%-99% equally spaced detection-probability lattice. The lattice is a design grid, not a survey of how often laboratory lot differences fall in each band. Archived validation files were then scored, in a convenience sample, against author-specified operating characteristics rather than against power targets written in those files.
